What earns its place in the toolbox

01 · OBD-II code reader

The basic tool for anyone working on a modern car. It reads and clears diagnostic trouble codes and identifies which system set them. A reader gives you the five-character code you need before you search our code library, and it lets you confirm that a repair actually fixed the fault.

Look for: reliable OBD-II/EOBD support, clear code descriptions, freeze-frame data and a readable screen. Live data is a bonus, not a requirement for a first tool.

Typical budget: about $20 – $60

02 · Scan tool with live data

When a code points to a sensor or a system, live data shows what that sensor is actually reporting while the engine runs. Comparing commanded and actual values is often the fastest way to separate a bad sensor from a wiring, vacuum or mechanical problem.

Look for: live data graphing, mode 6 test results, and support for the makes you actually work on. Advanced bidirectional controls are useful but cost more — buy them when you need them.

Typical budget: about $70 – $200

See live-data scan tools on Amazon →

03 · Digital multimeter

The cheapest way to test a circuit. Continuity checks find broken wiring, voltage-drop tests find high resistance, and resistance checks verify sensors. A code reader can tell you a circuit is out of range; a multimeter tells you why.

Look for: a robust input fuse, clear display, test leads in good condition, and a size that fits in your hand. Accuracy matters more than extra functions.

Typical budget: about $25 – $80

See digital multimeters on Amazon →

04 · Tyre pressure gauge

Underinflated tyres increase rolling resistance, wear out early and change handling. A reliable gauge is the simplest fuel-economy tool you can own, and it belongs in the glovebox rather than the workshop.

Look for: a gauge that reads in both kPa/bar and psi if you travel, a comfortable head, and a calm habit of checking pressures when the tyres are cold.

Typical budget: about $10 – $40

See tyre pressure gauges on Amazon →

05 · Vacuum and smoke test kit

Vacuum leaks and EVAP leaks cause rough idle, lean codes and failed emissions tests. A hand vacuum pump or a smoke machine lets you find the leak instead of replacing sensors one at a time.

Look for: a kit suited to the pressure ranges you need, quality fittings and a safe, controlled way to introduce smoke into the intake or EVAP system.

Typical budget: about $25 – $120

See vacuum and smoke test kits on Amazon →

06 · Fuel pressure test kit

Low fuel pressure can mimic a weak pump, a clogged filter, a bad regulator or an injector fault. A gauge on the rail tells you which direction to look before you spend money on parts.

Look for: adapters for your vehicle's test port, a gauge with a useful range, and a clear understanding of the spec for your engine — pressures vary widely.

Typical budget: about $30 – $100

See fuel pressure test kits on Amazon →

07 · ECU memory saver

Disconnecting a battery can wipe learned adaptive values, radio codes and window settings. A memory saver keeps a small supply to the ECU while you work, so you are not starting from scratch afterwards.

Look for: a unit that matches your vehicle's electrical system, short-circuit protection and clear instructions. Used incorrectly, any battery device can cause damage — follow the manual.

Typical budget: about $20 – $70

See ECU memory savers on Amazon →

08 · Scan tool with ABS/SRS coverage

Engine codes get most of the attention, but brakes, airbags and transmission modules set their own faults. A wider-coverage scan tool helps you see the system that actually reported the problem instead of guessing from symptoms alone.

Look for: explicit manufacturer coverage for the makes you work on, clear live-data support and the ability to read and clear codes in non-engine modules. Bidirectional tests are useful, but only if you understand what they do.

Typical budget: about $100 – $400

See ABS/SRS scan tools on Amazon →

09 · Battery tester / load tester

A weak battery can create strange electrical codes, slow cranking and intermittent module faults. Testing it first stops you from chasing sensors when the real problem is supply voltage.

Look for: a tester suited to your battery type, clear state-of-charge and health readings, and safe clamps. Always follow the tool instructions and take care around short circuits.

Typical budget: about $20 – $100

See battery testers on Amazon →

10 · Backprobe pin kit

Sensors and modules are easy to misdiagnose if you cannot safely measure a live circuit. Backprobe pins let a multimeter read a connector without piercing the wire insulation, which helps preserve the harness.

Look for: several pin sizes, flexible leads that fit your meter, and a set that reaches common connector shapes. Use gentle pressure and never force a pin into a sealed connector.

Typical budget: about $10 – $35

See backprobe kits on Amazon →

11 · Noid light / injector pulse tester

Injector-related codes and misfires can come from wiring, power supply or the control side of the circuit. A noid light shows whether the ECU is commanding the injector at all, which narrows the test before parts are replaced.

Look for: the connector types that match the vehicles you work on, a clear indicator and a kit with a storage case. It confirms pulse, not injector flow or spray pattern.

Typical budget: about $10 – $40

See injector pulse testers on Amazon →

12 · Engine compression tester

Misfire codes are not always electrical. A compression test checks the mechanical health of the engine and helps separate valve, ring and head-gasket problems from ignition or fuel faults.

Look for: the right spark-plug adapters for your vehicles, a gauge you can read clearly and a reliable schrader valve. Compare readings cylinder-to-cylinder, not just against one generic number.

Typical budget: about $25 – $90

See compression testers on Amazon →

13 · Cooling system pressure tester

Overheating, coolant loss and temperature-related codes are expensive if you guess. Pressurising the cooling system makes leaks show up before the engine gets hot, and it helps check that the cap holds pressure.

Look for: an adapter set that fits common radiator and expansion-tank necks, a gauge with a sensible range and a hand pump that feels smooth. Never open a hot cooling system.

Typical budget: about $30 – $120

See cooling pressure testers on Amazon →

14 · Infrared thermometer

Temperature differences tell a story. Comparing a thermostat housing, radiator inlet and outlet, or two sides of the same component, can point to a stuck thermostat, restriction or airflow problem without touching hot parts.

Look for: a consistent distance-to-spot ratio, clear display and a comfortable grip. Remember that shiny metal surfaces can give misleading readings, so use the same target surface each time.

Typical budget: about $15 – $50

See infrared thermometers on Amazon →

15 · OBD-II extension cable / right-angle adapter

The diagnostic port is often tucked under the dash in an awkward spot. An extension cable or right-angle adapter protects the vehicle socket, gives your scanner room to sit still, and makes long live-data sessions less frustrating.

Look for: a secure fit, enough length to reach your seating position and a shape that does not block nearby pedals or panels. It does not add features to a basic scanner — it simply makes the connection practical.

Typical budget: about $10 – $30

See OBD-II extension cables on Amazon →

After the diagnosis

Common replacement parts

Once a test points to a part, these are the usual wear items worth comparing. Confirm the fault and match the exact engine before you buy — a code names a circuit, not a doomed component.

16 · Oxygen sensor

Oxygen sensors report what is happening in the exhaust so the ECU can trim the fuel mixture. A failing one can trigger lean or rich codes, rough running and failed emissions tests — but those codes also point at leaks and wiring, so test the circuit before condemning the sensor.

Look for: the exact sensor type, wire count and connector for your vehicle, plus the correct upstream or downstream position. Universal splice-in sensors need careful wiring; a direct-fit part is simpler for most home mechanics.

Typical budget: about $20 – $90

See oxygen sensors on Amazon →

17 · Mass airflow sensor

The MAF sensor measures incoming air so the ECU can deliver the right amount of fuel. Contamination or a failed sensor can cause rough idle, stalling and lean codes. A leak in the intake after the sensor can produce the same symptoms, so check for unmetered air first.

Look for: an exact housing and connector match, the correct calibration for your engine, and a cleaner that is safe for your sensor type if you try cleaning before replacing.

Typical budget: about $30 – $150

See mass airflow sensors on Amazon →

18 · Spark plugs

Spark plugs are a routine wear item, but the wrong heat range or gap can cause misfires, hard starting and poor economy. A misfire code is not automatically a plug fault — coils, injectors and engine compression can produce the same symptom.

Look for: the plug type, heat range and gap specified for your engine, the correct thread reach, and a reputable seller. Even pre-gapped plugs deserve a quick gap check, and tighten to the manual's torque specification.

Typical budget: about $15 – $60 per set

See spark plugs on Amazon →

19 · Ignition coils

Coils turn low voltage into the spark that lights the air-fuel mixture. A weak coil often shows up as a misfire under load. On coil-on-plug engines you can sometimes swap a suspect coil to another cylinder to see whether the misfire follows it — a free test that beats guessing.

Look for: a coil that matches the exact engine and connector, and a reputable source. Confirm the fault before replacing a whole set, and inspect the plug and boot at the same time.

Typical budget: about $20 – $100

See ignition coils on Amazon →

20 · Engine air filter

A clean air filter protects the engine and keeps intake flow free. A badly clogged one can restrict airflow and affect economy, but it rarely causes a dramatic fault on its own. Inspect it before buying — a filter that only looks dusty may still be serviceable.

Look for: the correct panel or drop-in shape for your airbox and a filter that seals properly in the housing. A poor fit lets unfiltered air straight into the engine, which is worse than a slightly dirty filter.

Typical budget: about $10 – $30

See engine air filters on Amazon →

21 · Engine oil

The right grade and specification matter more than the brand on the bottle. Using the wrong viscosity or missing a required specification can affect wear, fuel economy and emissions hardware. Your owner's manual is the authority here, not a generic chart.

Look for: the viscosity grade and specification listed for your engine, the correct quantity for a change, and a reputable seller. Check the container for the relevant API or ACEA marking before you buy.

Typical budget: about $20 – $60 per change

See engine oils on Amazon →

22 · Battery charger / maintainer

A smart charger or maintainer keeps a battery healthy during storage and can recover a lightly discharged one. It complements the battery tester above — charging a battery is not the same as knowing whether it still holds charge and health.

Look for: a charger matched to your battery type (including AGM or EFB where applicable), an automatic float or maintain mode, and reverse-polarity protection. Always follow the safety instructions and work in a ventilated area.

Typical budget: about $25 – $120

See battery chargers on Amazon →
